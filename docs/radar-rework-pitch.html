Diagnosis

Why it feels choppy today

The current layer is smarter than it looks — client-side palette remapping, a rolling dissolve, nowcast frames — but it fights its own architecture in three places, and all three are visible on screen.

Two of these we verified in Cesium 1.121's own source: layers with show: true fetch, decode, and upload tiles regardless of alpha, and a newly loaded globe tile isn't renderable until every layer's imagery for it is in — so with 15+ radar layers alive, every camera pan waits on fifteen tile pyramids, and frame textures hold 200–500 MB of VRAM at deeper zooms, on hardware this codebase already documents as context-loss-prone. That is the choppiness, mechanically. (To be fair to the original design: the eager 15-layer preload is what makes scrubbing instant — any replacement has to keep that property by smarter means, not just delete it.)

There's also a perceptual reason the dissolve can't be saved by tuning: storms advect 6–15 km per 10-minute frame — 10–25 screen pixels at regional zooms. Crossfading across a displacement wider than the cell itself produces the classic double-exposure artifact: each cell fades out in place while its ghost fades in ahead. Motion reads as pulsing teleportation no matter how well-fed the layers are. Only warping along the storm's actual motion fixes it — that's the line between Option 1 and Option 2.

The other shoe

The foundation is shrinking under us

Part of "not particularly valuable right now" isn't our code at all. RainViewer discontinued most of its free API in January 2026 — and there is no paid tier to upgrade to; they exited the API business entirely. Per their transition notes:

  • Nowcast/forecast frames: discontinued. The forecast half of our timeline is fed by a product that no longer exists.
  • Infrared satellite: discontinued. The "Clouds" and "Combined" modes have nothing to render.
  • All color schemes but one: discontinued. The CDN ignoring our palette parameter — the thing the palette-inversion hack works around — is permanent, not a glitch.
  • Max tile zoom clamped to level 7. The client still requests levels 8–9 (RADAR_MAX_LEVEL = 9), which would explain tiles that never arrive when zoomed in.
  • 100 requests/IP/min rate limit — a number a 16-layer rebuild storm can plausibly hit on its own.

(Sourced from RainViewer's transition FAQ and API docs; this sandbox can't reach their endpoints directly, so the zoom clamp and rate limit should be re-verified from production before we lean on them.) The takeaway either way: any rework that treats RainViewer as a permanent foundation is building on sand. The options below are ordered by how far they step off of it.

Reference point

What zoom.earth is actually doing

We reverse-engineered their radar stack from public integrations against their endpoints. The punchline: everything hard happens on their servers, and the browser's job is made trivial.

  • They pre-process radar into their own tile pyramid. A tiny manifest (times/radar.json) maps each frame timestamp to a content hash; tiles live at /radar/reflectivity/{date}/{time}/{hash}/{z}/{y}/{x}.webp — small, pre-colored, display-ready 256px WebP. No client-side palette math at all.
  • Content-hash URLs make every frame immutable — cacheable forever at the CDN and in the browser. Replaying the loop costs zero refetches. This is most of why their loop feels instant.
  • The animation is modest — a ~12-frame loop with a rolling dissolve, refreshed every 10 minutes. The smoothness comes from tiles that are always already there, low-res data bilinearly upscaled (radar is intentionally capped coarse), and labels kept crisp above the weather.
  • Their data: NOAA MRMS for the US plus national radar services (Météo-France, UK Met Office, KMA, AEMET, and ~a dozen more), with a separate DWD ICON/GFS model layer for global precipitation forecasts. They don't blend satellite-derived rain into the radar mosaic — where radar doesn't exist, radar just ends.
  • They bootstrapped exactly the way we did. Until mid-2025, zoom.earth's rain overlay was literally RainViewer tiles fetched client-side. Then they built the pipeline above. Our Option 3 is, more or less, the move they made.

House rules

What any option must preserve

From a full read of the integration surface — these are the contracts a redesign breaks at its peril:

  • The stacking order. Weather sits above the basemap and below place labels (addImageryBelowLabels) — radar blotting out city names is the fastest way to make the view illegible.
  • Explicit rendering. The globe runs requestRenderMode; every visual change must call requestRender() or it silently doesn't appear until the camera moves.
  • Context-loss discipline. The app has an elaborate WebGL context-loss recovery path; every async tile callback, worker completion, and animation tick must guard viewer.isDestroyed().
  • The crisis share page. RadarLayer + RadarTimeline mount standalone on a public, anonymous share view — tile fetching must stay keyless and client-side there. (This also rules out browser-direct keyed commercial feeds.)
  • Timeline coupling. EarthTimeBar repositions itself by subscribing to the radar timeline's shape — the store's exports are a public interface.
  • Instant scrubbing. Today's eager preload is what makes scrubbing feel immediate; its replacement is a prefetch horizon, not deletion.

Directions

Three ways to rebuild it

They are not mutually exclusive — each one contains the previous. The question is where to stop, and the mockups below let you feel the difference before committing.

OPTION 1

The Refit

Keep RainViewer and the current look; replace the plumbing. Two imagery layers instead of sixteen, all pixel work off the main thread, and nothing ever refetched without reason. Playback still steps every 10 minutes — but it loads fast and never stutters.

EFFORT ~1–2 wk RISK LOW NEW INFRA NONE MOTION 10-MIN STEPS
LOOP CACHED 16/16

What changes

  • Two-layer ping-pong. Only the visible frame and the incoming frame exist as Cesium layers; frames swap in place via the provider's time-templated reload — the same flicker-free hook Cesium's own time-dynamic WMTS uses, which keeps old tile textures on screen until replacements are ready. The ×16 pyramid stream disappears.
  • Worker recolor. Decode → invert → smooth → palette runs in a Web Worker pool on OffscreenCanvas; tiles arrive as ready ImageBitmaps. Zero main-thread pixel work.
  • Decoded-field cache. The inverted dBZ field is cached per tile — a palette switch re-maps from cache instead of refetching the internet.
  • Loop warming. The exact tile set the radar layer is rendering is readable off the globe surface — prefetch those URLs for every frame in the loop before autoplay begins; the play button shows a progress ring until the loop is airtight.
  • Manifest diffing. New frames append; old frames drop. No more full teardown every poll.

What it can't fix

  • Motion is still 10-minute keyframes with a dissolve — better fed, not smoother.
  • Still hostage to RainViewer's fixed served palette and the inversion hack — and to a provider that just exited the API business. The refit also has to prune what upstream killed: drop the dead Clouds/Combined modes and forecast frames, align RADAR_MAX_LEVEL to the new zoom-7 cap.
  • No cadence upgrade: CONUS stays at 10 min while MRMS publishes every 2.
OPTION 2

The Motion Engine

RECOMMENDED

Everything in the Refit, plus radar stops being a stack of pictures and becomes data on the GPU: two keyframes and a motion field, blended per-pixel by a shader at any instant in time. Storms drift continuously instead of dissolving in place — the thing zoom.earth's loop has and ours doesn't.

EFFORT ~3–5 wk RISK MEDIUM NEW INFRA NONE MOTION CONTINUOUS
FLOW-INTERPOLATED

What changes (on top of Option 1)

  • GPU weather pass. Radar frames upload as raw dBZ data textures; a fragment shader maps them through a 1-D palette LUT texture and blends two keyframes per pixel. Palette switching becomes free and instant. The proven shape is a hybrid: a globe-draped material pass at regional-to-global zooms (Cesium materials accept swappable ImageBitmap sampler uniforms), falling back to the Stage A ping-pong layers when zoomed deep. Stage B proves it before we commit.
  • Optical-flow interpolation. A worker computes a coarse motion field between consecutive mosaics (pyramidal Lucas-Kanade on a downsampled luminance mosaic: ~30–300 ms per frame-pair, amortized in the background); the shader warps frame A forward and frame B backward along it — storms move between keyframes instead of crossfading. Per displayed frame the warp costs ~3 texture taps: <1 ms of GPU.
  • Proven, not exotic. Dark Sky shipped exactly this (server velocity textures, client warp), and an open-source Home Assistant weather card runs worker-side Lucas-Kanade over RainViewer tiles in production today. Because both endpoints of every blend are observed truth, even mediocre flow looks right.
  • The forecast comes back. RainViewer killed its nowcast in Jan 2026 — but the same flow field, extrapolated forward, regenerates a +30–60 min advection nowcast on the client. We rebuild upstream's dead feature ourselves, clearly flagged as forecast.
  • A continuous timeline. Time is a float, not an index of 16. Scrubbing is perfectly fluid; playback speed becomes a control, not a constant.
  • Data diversification, zero infra. Iowa Environmental Mesonet serves ready-made NEXRAD/MRMS composite tiles at 5-min cadence for CONUS — a free, no-key second source the engine can prefer over RainViewer where it's better, straight from the browser.
  • Graceful fallback. If flow fails or WebGL context is lost, the engine degrades to Option 1's dissolve — never to a blank layer.

What it can't fix

  • Interpolation is perceptual, not forecast-grade — between-frame motion is a reconstruction, flagged as such in the UI.
  • Global coverage still leans on RainViewer's shrunken free tier (10-min, past-only, zoom 7); IEM is a university service run on goodwill, not an SLA.
  • No real snow/mix typing — that needs Option 3's data.
  • The custom Cesium render path is the risk concentration — it needs a spike week before committing.
OPTION 3

The First-Party Pipeline

The Motion Engine, fed by our own data. A server-side ingest worker turns NOAA MRMS (2-minute, 1-km CONUS) and a global mosaic into first-party data tiles with motion fields and precipitation type — the layer stops being a RainViewer skin and becomes an owned capability.

EFFORT ~6–10 wk RISK HIGH NEW INFRA WORKER + OBJECT STORAGE MOTION CONTINUOUS · 2-MIN DATA
MRMS 2-MIN · CELL TRACKING

What changes (on top of Option 2)

  • MRMS ingest — verified viable this week. NOAA's MRMS mirror on AWS S3 publishes CONUS composites every 2 minutes, ~1–2.5 min behind real time, 0.2–1.6 MB gzipped GRIB2 per frame, Access-Control-Allow-Origin: *, public domain. A scheduled worker decodes and emits data-encoded imagery — real dBZ, no palette inversion, 5× the temporal resolution. At this size, ingest is <5% of one vCPU.
  • The CONUS shortcut. MRMS is one 7000×3500 grid — the ingest can emit a single value-encoded WebP per frame and drape it as one Cesium imagery rectangle. No tile pyramid, no tile server: object storage + CDN and done. (Content-hash URLs, immutable forever — zoom.earth's exact caching trick. Mobile GPUs cap textures at 4096px, so ship a half-res variant or a 2×2 split for them.)
  • Global merge. RainViewer (or per-country radars, or a commercial feed like Rainbow.ai's raw-dBZ tiles) fills the rest of the world — one seamless field with per-region provenance.
  • Precip typing. MRMS PrecipFlag distinguishes rain / snow / mix — the snow ramp in the palette finally gets real data.
  • Server-side flow fields. Motion vectors precompute once per frame-pair for everyone, instead of per-client.
  • Storm-cell layer. Cell detection on the ingest side unlocks tracked cells with motion vectors and intensity trends as a click-able overlay.

What it costs

  • Real infrastructure: a worker process, object storage/CDN, monitoring — on an app that today runs on one 512 MB Heroku dyno that also hosts AIS/lightning collectors. Decoding a 7000×3500 grid peaks at 100–200 MB, so ingest lives on a worker process or a small VPS, not the web dyno. (The single-image drape trick shrinks the serving side, but doesn't erase ops.)
  • An operational commitment: MRMS outages, format drift, and storage growth become our pages, not RainViewer's.
  • Longest time-to-value; nothing ships until the pipeline is trustworthy. Prior art exists to lean on (LibreWXR, an AGPL self-hosted RainViewer-compatible pipeline), but it's still ours to run.

Recommendation

Option 2, staged to pay out early

Build the Motion Engine — but ship the Refit on the way.

Option 1 alone fixes the complaints but leaves the experience a slideshow built on a provider that's winding down; Option 3 is the right long-term destination but stakes weeks on infrastructure before anyone sees a pixel improve. Option 2 hits the quality bar that motivated this rework — motion indistinguishable from zoom.earth's — with zero new infrastructure, it resurrects the forecast RainViewer killed, and it's built in stages that each ship standalone value. Critically, its GPU engine consumes data textures + flow fields — exactly what Option 3's pipeline would emit — so when 2-minute MRMS or precip typing earn priority, the pipeline slots in under an unchanged renderer. That's the path zoom.earth itself took: they rendered RainViewer tiles until mid-2025, then swapped their own pipeline in behind an established client. Choosing 2 keeps 3 open; choosing 1 doesn't.
